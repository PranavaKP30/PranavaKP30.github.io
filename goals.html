Career goals

Where I'm headed

My goal is to become a hardware engineer who designs products people rely on, and eventually to lead the hardware for them.

The work I enjoy most combines analog circuits, power electronics, and embedded systems, and has to work reliably every time. Research taught me how to build hardware that's safe to put on a person; industry is where I'll learn how to turn that into a product. I want to build that career here in the Boston area.

The roadmap

  • 2026: research foundationsStart at Georgia Tech. Own the hardware for the Neuroloops tactile restoration wearable, from schematics and flex PCB layout to firmware, and keep building real-time closed-loop systems at Northeastern's Movement Neuroscience Lab.
  • 2027: first industry experienceComplete a hardware co-op or internship in Boston and learn how products go from prototype to production. Keep pushing the Neuroloops system through testing and the FPGA processor, and build my core foundation in circuits, digital design, and electronics.
  • 2028: depthA second industry role, plus upper-level coursework in analog electronics, op-amp design, biomedical instrumentation, and DSP. Bring my research projects to a finished, published state.
  • 2029: full-time hardware engineerGraduate and join a hardware team full time, owning boards and subsystems on a product that ships.
  • Long term: hardware leadGrow into a technical lead role, owning the hardware architecture for a product and mentoring the engineers who build it.

How I'll get there

  • Research: keep taking full ownership of real systems in the lab, from first schematic to tested hardware.
  • Industry work: co-ops and internships teach design for manufacturing, testing, and working on a team that ships, which research can't.
  • Coursework: use my classes to understand the theory behind design choices I'm already making by hand (see my plan below).
  • Personal projects: build things on my own, like my EMG module, to try ideas fast and learn without a safety net.

Why these threads: the upper-level courses line up directly with what I already build. Analog Electronics and Operational Amplifier Design go deeper into the front ends on my EMG and EEG boards, Biomedical Instrumentation and Engineering Electrophysiology cover the physics of the signals I record, and DSP and Signals and Systems sit behind the real-time processing in my lab work.

If I take a co-op: I'm applying for Spring 2027 hardware co-ops in Boston. If I take one, every semester after Fall 2026 shifts back by one term and I'd graduate in May 2029 instead of December 2028. That trade is worth it to me: a semester of real product work early on will make every course after it more useful.
