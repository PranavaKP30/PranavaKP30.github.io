Tactile Restoration Wearable

Undergraduate Researcher · Sept. 2026 to present · Hardware design and embedded firmware

The Problem

People who lose feeling in their hands from injury or amputation lose a big part of how they interact with things. The lab is building a wearable that reads the body's signals (EEG and EMG), runs them through a spiking neural network, and uses electrical stimulation on the skin to create a sense of touch. I'm responsible for the hardware.

The Process

I designed the whole system myself, from part selection and schematics to 4-layer flex PCB layout with stiffened areas under the components. It's split into modules so pieces can be swapped out:

  • Acquisition modules: 8-channel EEG/EMG boards built around the ADS1299, each 65 × 38 mm. Two of them run off a shared clock to get 16 channels.
  • Stimulation hub: a galvanically isolated, ~90V biphasic stimulator. It uses a boost converter and an H-bridge with a hardware interlock so both sides can't turn on at once, plus current limiting.
  • Fault protection: a hardware latch shuts off stimulation on overcurrent, bad electrode impedance, over-temperature, or a supply brownout. An analog switch also disconnects the recording inputs during each pulse so the stimulation doesn't wreck them. None of this depends on firmware.
  • Firmware (in progress): synchronized acquisition, isolated SPI/I2C control of the stimulator, live impedance measurement, and adjusting the stimulation waveform based on it.

The Outcome

The hardware design is done and I'm working on firmware now. The biggest thing I took from this project is to put the safety-critical stuff in hardware. If the firmware crashes, the user should still be safe.

This is active lab research, so I can only share a high-level view of the design.
