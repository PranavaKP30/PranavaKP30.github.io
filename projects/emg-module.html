Single-Channel EMG Acquisition Module

Personal project · Fall 2026 · Analog front end, PCB design, ESP32 firmware

The Problem

When a muscle contracts it puts out a small electrical signal you can measure on the skin. The problem is that it's only a few millivolts at most, and there's a lot of noise from power lines, movement, and other electronics on top of it. Lab EMG systems deal with this fine, but they're expensive and not portable. I wanted to see what it takes to build a small board that gets a clean muscle signal and streams it to a computer in real time.

The Process

Pretty much every biosignal system has the same stages: protect the input, amplify the difference between two electrodes, filter out what you don't need, digitize it, and send the data somewhere. I designed the board around those stages.

  • Input protection: series resistors and clamp diodes protect the circuit from static and spikes, and RC filters cut high-frequency noise before the amplifier.
  • Analog front end: a differential amplifier cancels out noise that shows up on both electrodes and amplifies the actual muscle signal. Then filtering limits it to the frequency range EMG is in. EDIT: name the front-end chip or op-amp circuit, its gain, and filter cutoffs.
  • Firmware: an ESP32 samples the signal at a fixed rate, buffers it, and streams it to my laptop where I plot it live.
  • PCB: I did the schematic and layout in KiCad. I kept the analog section away from the digital and power parts and used a solid ground plane to keep noise down.

The biggest thing I learned was how much layout matters. Trace routing, ground return paths, and where the decoupling caps go can decide whether you see a clean muscle signal or just 60 Hz noise.

The Outcome

This board is basically a single-channel version of the 16-channel EEG/EMG modules I'm designing at the Neuroloops Lab. A lot of what I tested here, like the input protection and keeping analog and digital separate, went straight into that design.

What I'd do next

I'd add more channels, improve the shielding and grounding to lower the noise floor, and use the signal to control something, like a gripper that copies your hand movement.
