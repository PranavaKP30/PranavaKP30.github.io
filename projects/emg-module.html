Single-Channel EMG Acquisition Module

Personal project · Fall 2026 · Analog front end, PCB design, ESP32 firmware

The Problem

When a muscle contracts, it produces an electrical signal you can measure on the skin, but it's only a few millivolts at best and it's buried in noise from power lines, motion, and nearby electronics. Research-grade EMG systems handle this well, but they're expensive and tied to a desk. I wanted to understand what it actually takes to build a small, standalone module that captures a clean muscle signal and streams it to a computer in real time.

The Process

I broke the design into the same stages every biosignal system needs: protect the input, amplify the difference between two electrodes, filter out what you don't want, digitize it, and get the data out.

  • Input protection: series resistors and clamp diodes protect the front end from static and spikes, and RC filters knock down high-frequency noise before it reaches the amplifier.
  • Analog front end: a differential amplifier rejects the noise both electrodes share and amplifies the muscle signal, then filtering band-limits it to the range EMG actually lives in. EDIT: name the front-end chip or op-amp circuit, its gain, and filter cutoffs.
  • Firmware: an ESP32 samples the signal at a fixed rate, buffers it, and streams it to a computer, where I plot it live.
  • PCB design: I drew the schematic and laid out the board in KiCad, keeping the analog section away from digital and power circuitry and using a solid ground plane to keep noise down.

The biggest lesson was how much layout matters. Where a trace runs, how ground currents return, and where decoupling capacitors sit can be the difference between a clean muscle burst and a screen full of 60 Hz noise.

The Outcome

This board is the simple, single-channel version of the 16-channel EEG/EMG acquisition modules I'm designing at the Neuroloops Lab, and a lot of the choices I tested here, from input protection to keeping analog and digital apart, carry straight into that system.

What I'd do next

Next, I'd add more channels, improve shielding and grounding for a lower noise floor, and use the signal to drive something physical, like a gripper that mirrors the motion of your hand.
