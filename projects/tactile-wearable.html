Neuromorphic Tactile Restoration Wearable

Undergraduate Researcher · Sept. 2026 to present · Hardware design and embedded firmware

A closed-loop hybrid BCI wearable that restores the sense of touch by recording EEG and EMG, decoding them with a spiking neural network, and modulating electrical stimulation of the skin to evoke tactile sensation.

The Problem

People who lose feeling in their hands from nerve injury or amputation lose the feedback they rely on to grip, hold, and handle objects. Electrical stimulation of the skin's mechanoreceptors can activate peripheral nerves and create a sense of touch, but most stimulators deliver fixed patterns and have no way of responding to what the body is doing. The goal of this project is a wearable that closes that loop: it reads the user's neural and muscle activity and adjusts stimulation in real time.

The Solution

I own the hardware for the system end to end, from part selection and schematics through 4-layer flex PCB layout and the embedded firmware. The spiking neural network (SNN) and the experiment platform used to run sessions are developed by other members of the lab.

The system is split into modules so each piece can be designed, tested, and swapped independently:

  • Modular flex design: 4-layer flex PCBs with stiffened component islands so the system can sit on the forearm, connected by custom flex FPC interconnects.
  • Acquisition: interchangeable 8-channel ADS1299 modules, each 65 × 38 mm, configurable for EEG or EMG. Two modules run off a shared clock for 16 synchronized channels.
  • Stimulation: a galvanically isolated, ~90V biphasic TENS stimulator built from a boost converter and an H-bridge, with a hardware shoot-through interlock and current limiting.
  • Safety in hardware: a fault latch cuts stimulation on overcurrent, out-of-range electrode impedance, over-temperature, or supply brownout, and stimulation-triggered analog switches disconnect the EEG/EMG front ends during each pulse. Both work independently of firmware.
  • Firmware (in development): synchronized 16-channel acquisition and real-time signal processing, isolated SPI/I2C control of the stimulation hub, electrode impedance calculated from live current and voltage sensing, impedance-adaptive biphasic waveforms, and fault monitoring and recovery.

Right now, the SNN runs separately from the wearable, and its stimulation paradigms are loaded into the firmware manually. The experiment platform communicates with the firmware to run and control each session.

Impact and Status

The hardware design is complete, and the firmware is in development. Once finished, the wearable gives the lab a platform for studying closed-loop sensory restoration outside of a traditional benchtop setup, with recording and stimulation on the same body-worn system.

The biggest lesson from this project has been to put safety-critical behavior in hardware. Stimulation and microvolt-level recording sit on the same system, so protection can't depend on firmware running correctly. If the microcontroller crashes, the user still has to be safe.

Future Plans

The next step is moving the SNN onto the wearable itself. I'm working toward a custom neuromorphic processor that runs the SNN on an FPGA, including the RTL design and board bring-up. With decoding running on the device in real time, the firmware can handle closed-loop, EEG/EMG-modulated TENS stimulation directly, without manually loading paradigms.

This is active lab research, so I can only share a high-level view of the design here. I'm happy to go into more detail in an interview.
