EEG PCB Module with Firmware

Fall 2026 · Georgia Tech · PCB design, analog front end, ESP32 firmware

The Problem

EEG signals are tiny. Brain activity measured on the scalp is only tens of microvolts, which is thousands of times smaller than the noise from power lines, muscles, and nearby electronics. Research-grade EEG systems handle this well, but they are expensive, bulky, and tied to a desk. I wanted to find out what it takes to build a small, standalone EEG module that can capture clean brain signals and stream them to a computer in real time, and to use it as a stepping stone toward the wearable hardware I'm building in the lab.

The Process

I broke the design into the same stages every biosignal system needs: protect the input, amplify and filter the signal, digitize it, and get the data out.

  • Input protection and filtering: series resistors and clamp diodes protect the front end from static and spikes, and RC filters remove high-frequency noise before it reaches the amplifier.
  • Analog front end: the signal is amplified and band-limited so the ADC sees a clean, usable range. EDIT: name the front-end chip or op-amp circuit you used, its gain, and filter cutoffs.
  • Digitization and firmware: an ESP32 samples the data at a fixed rate, buffers it, and streams it to a computer, where I plot it live.
  • PCB design: I drew the schematic and laid out the board in KiCad, keeping the analog section away from the digital and power circuitry and using solid ground planes to reduce noise.

The biggest lesson in this stage was how much layout matters. Where a trace runs, how ground returns flow, and where decoupling capacitors sit can make the difference between a clean EEG trace and a screen full of 60 Hz noise.

The Outcome

This board became the earlier version of the 16-channel acquisition modules I'm now designing at the Neuroloops Lab. Many of the design choices I tested here, including input protection, filtering, and keeping analog and digital domains separate, carried directly into that system.

What I'd do next

Next, I'd add more channels, improve the shielding and grounding for lower noise, and move to a fully wireless, battery-powered design that someone could actually wear.
