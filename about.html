About me

Hi, I'm Pranav.

I'm an Electrical Engineering student at Georgia Tech in the Circuit Technology and Bioengineering threads, graduating in December 2028. Most of what I build sits right where electronics meet the human body.

At Georgia Tech's Neuroloops Lab, I own the hardware for a wearable that restores the sense of touch. I picked the parts, drew the schematics, and laid out the 4-layer flex PCBs: 8-channel EEG/EMG acquisition modules that pick up microvolt signals, and an isolated ~90V stimulator that shuts itself down in hardware the moment something goes wrong. Now I'm writing its firmware and planning an FPGA processor to run the lab's spiking neural network in real time.

I'm also part of Northeastern's Movement Neuroscience Lab, where I started as a research intern and stayed on as a visiting researcher. There I write real-time C++ on Xenomai Linux: device drivers for TMS and EMG systems, a closed-loop platform that tunes brain stimulation from live EEG, and a firmware interface for a clinical stimulator. Watching my code run on real participants made reliability, timing, and safety non-negotiable for me.

What I enjoy most is the part where analog circuits, power, and software all have to agree. That's usually where the hardest bugs live, and it's the kind of problem I want to keep solving, whether the product is a medical device, a wearable, or anything else that has to just work.

What I bring

  • Hardware design: schematics, 4-layer and flex PCB layout, analog front ends, isolated power and stimulation circuits
  • Embedded and real-time software: C++, Python, PlatformIO, ESP32, Xenomai real-time Linux
  • Communication protocols: SPI, I2C, UART, TCP/IP
  • Lab skills: oscilloscopes, multimeters, power supplies, board bring-up, and hardware validation

Outside of engineering

I tutor on Schoolhouse.world, a free peer-tutoring platform, where I'm in the top 5% of tutors globally. I've run 47 sessions for 32 learners and mentored 105 other tutors, and explaining a concept to someone new is still one of the best ways I've found to actually understand it.
